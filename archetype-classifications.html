Archetype Classifications

Role: UX Designer & Developer | Duration: 4 weeks | Tools: Figma | Team: 3 members

1. Overview

This project explored pedestrian behavior at crosswalks through the lens of an urban “trolley problem.” Our goal was to identify and classify behavioral archetypes that reveal how people negotiate mobility, safety, and social signals in shared urban spaces.

2. Context / Problem

Pedestrian crossings are a critical point of interaction between people, vehicles, and the built environment. Yet, individual behaviors at crosswalks can vary widely — from assertive crossings to cautious hesitations — often influencing how traffic flow and safety unfold.

Our team sought to better understand these patterns to inform urban design decisions and potentially inspire more human-centered mobility systems. By observing real-world intersections, we aimed to uncover how pedestrians prioritize safety, efficiency, and social awareness in context.

3. Research & Insights

We conducted field observations of pedestrians at several intersections, documenting variables such as mobility type, group size, attentiveness, distractions, and crossing timing.

From our observations, several archetypes emerged — such as The Leader (assertive and time-efficient), The Follower (dependent on group cues), and The Hesitant (risk-averse and reactive).

  • People in groups tend to follow a single decision-maker rather than individually assessing safety.
  • Phone use and social engagement often delayed crossings or created unpredictable pauses.
  • Pedestrian assertiveness increased with familiarity of the intersection.

An “aha” moment came when we realized these distinct patterns could be systematically classified — paving the way for a taxonomy of pedestrian behavior useful for design and policy applications.

4. Ideation

We brainstormed ways to visualize behavioral archetypes clearly and meaningfully. Using mind mapping and sketching, we explored frameworks for clustering observations based on traits like decision confidence, distraction, and risk-taking.

Through iterative discussions, we aligned on using behavioral classification visuals that balanced data clarity with intuitive storytelling.

5. Design & Prototyping

Using Figma, we created a digital visualization system that categorized observed pedestrian behaviors into visually distinct archetypes.

We began with low-fidelity grids of behavioral dimensions (e.g., attention vs. assertiveness) and advanced to high-fidelity prototypes that layered motion, color, and annotation to emphasize distinctions.

Design Rationale

  • Color: Warm tones indicated high assertiveness; cooler tones represented cautious or passive behavior.
  • Layout: Cluster diagrams reflected proximity and overlap of behavioral types.
  • Typography: Clean sans-serif text for clarity and legibility across screens.

Accessibility was also considered by ensuring sufficient color contrast and including descriptive labels for each archetype.

6. Testing & Iteration

We conducted peer feedback sessions to evaluate clarity, interpretability, and visual balance.

Test participants were able to correctly interpret the classifications over 80% of the time but noted that color meanings needed clearer legends.

After iteration, we refined the hierarchy, simplified the visual scale, and added concise explanatory text to strengthen comprehension.

7. Outcome / Impact

The final prototype successfully communicated the diversity of pedestrian crossing behaviors and offered a framework for behavioral archetype classification in urban contexts.

This approach could inform future work in urban analytics, UX for mobility systems, or human-centered traffic design.

The project strengthened our ability to merge qualitative observation with visual system design, and demonstrated how human behavior analysis can influence design thinking beyond digital interfaces.

8. Reflection

This project taught me to observe and represent behavior with both empathy and structure. If I were to revisit it, I’d expand the dataset with video analysis or cross-city comparisons to strengthen classification validity.

I’m most proud of how our team transformed messy, real-world observations into a structured, design-forward visualization that speaks to both urban design and user experience principles.
